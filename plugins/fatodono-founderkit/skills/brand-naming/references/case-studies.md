# Case Studies

Twelve worked examples. Each is structured **Situation → Insight → Construction → Outcome → Lesson**,
because the transferable thing is almost never the word — it's the move that produced it.

Read two or three that resemble the current problem before generating. Pattern-matching to a real
case beats reasoning from principles in the abstract.

---

## Swiffer (Procter & Gamble)

**Situation.** P&G had invented a genuinely new cleaning device and was describing it as a "new mop."
The names they wanted — `ProMop`, `EasyMop` — weren't available, and a competitor had already
shipped `ReadyMop`.

**Insight.** The researchers interviewed 25 people about their cleaning routines. Nobody liked mopping. So
the entire descriptive direction was building a brand on top of a chore people resent. The brief
flipped from *describe the device* to *change how the act feels*: quick, light, almost fun.

**Construction.** One team explored toys and vacuums (nothing to do with mops). Linguists studied
the sound of cleaning actions — sweep, wipe, wash, swipe, swift. `Swiffer` fuses the `sw-` of swift
motion with a light front vowel and a soft `-er` agent ending. It sounds like a verb you already
know.

**Outcome.** Chosen by 80%+ of consumers in research as the brand they'd most want to try. Became a
~$5B brand. `ReadyMop` — the accurate, descriptive, safe name — stayed small.

**Lesson.** When research says the category is disliked, describing the product accurately is the
worst available move. Name the feeling you want to install, not the object.

## Pentium (Intel)

**Situation.** Intel's processors were generation numbers — 386, 486. Numbers can't be trademarked,
so AMD shipped a 486 too and consumers saw two identical products. Intel needed a name that could be
owned, and one that could ride the "Intel Inside" campaign.

**Insight.** The strategic direction the researchers set was that the name should "sound and look like a
fundamental element of computing" — not like a model, a part, or a feature.

**Construction.** `penta-` (five — the fifth generation) + `-ium`, the suffix of sodium, uranium,
titanium. The `-ium` does the real work: it borrows the grammar of the periodic table, so the chip
reads as a basic substance rather than a component. Initial **p** stop gives recall; the vowels are
front and quick.

**Outcome.** Intel's executive committee split — one faction wanted the safe `ProChip`. Andy Grove
read the split as energy and chose Pentium. Within months consumers were asking for "a Pentium" by
name, something no one ever did for a 486.

**Lesson.** Two lessons. First, the root graft: borrow the morphology of a domain that already has
the credibility you want. Second, polarization inside the decision room is a buy signal, not a
warning. Also note Placek's counter to the standard objection: *invented names like Pentium take
less money to build into brands than existing words, not more.*

## BlackBerry (Research In Motion)

**Situation.** A handheld wireless device whose entire promise was immediacy — mail in your pocket,
right now.

**Insight.** Someone looked at the tiny keyboard buttons and said they looked like seeds:
"Strawberry." Will Leben killed it on phonetic grounds — `straw-` is a *slow* syllable, exactly
opposite to the zippy connotation RIM was selling.

**Construction.** But `-berry` was right: the researchers' work had **b** reading as reliable and the
short **e** reading as fast. So the requirement became mechanical — find another b-plus-short-vowel
syllable. Within seconds: `BlackBerry`. Compound of two known words, both globally legible,
"black" traveling across markets and "berry" keeping it approachable.

**Outcome.** The client initially resisted; competitors were not brave enough to name a serious
enterprise device after fruit, which is precisely what left the territory open.

**Lesson.** This is the clearest demonstration of the method working as engineering: a rejected
candidate is diagnosed at the *syllable* level, the defective syllable is replaced against explicit
phonetic criteria, and the answer falls out in seconds. Diagnose sounds, not names.

## Dasani (Coca-Cola)

**Situation.** Bottled water — a category with nothing to describe. The product is water. Every
functional claim is unavailable or meaningless.

**Insight.** With nothing to say, the name has to *be* the product experience. So the sound strategy
was written before any word existed: crisp, pure, healthy, light.

**Construction.** Assembled from three charged parts: `da-` (a stop onset delivering a crisp, clean
attack), `san` (the Latin root for health, familiar through sanitary/sane/sanitation without being
literal), and a final `-i` (front vowel — light, thin, slim). Nothing in the name means "water," and
everything in it sounds like the water you'd want.

**Outcome.** A billion-dollar brand in a category with no product differentiation.

**Lesson.** The purest example of designing the sound first and finding the letters second. When the
category offers no rational claim, phonetics *is* the positioning.

## Azure (Microsoft)

**Situation.** Microsoft's cloud platform, launching into a category about to become enormous and
crowded.

**Insight.** People inside Microsoft called it "a dumb name." That reaction is the tell — it means
the name isn't a rearrangement of what the room already expected.

**Construction.** A transplanted real word: a color, and specifically *sky* — semantic territory
nobody in cloud computing had claimed, delivered without ever saying "cloud." Phonetically it splits
the job: the **z** arrests attention (the researchers' attention letter), while `-zure` rhymes with "sure,"
smuggling in reliability for an enterprise buyer who needs exactly that.

**Outcome.** A multi-billion-dollar brand.

**Lesson.** "Familiar enough to process, surprising enough to stand out" in one word. And early
internal hostility to a name correlates with distinctiveness, not with being wrong.

## Windsurf (Codeium)

**Situation.** An AI coding IDE named Codeium — unrecognizable, hard to spell, weak in search, and
descriptive in the worst way (`code` + a suffix), so it blended into a field of code-something
competitors.

**Insight.** The breakthrough came from the *displaced* team — the one that didn't know software was
involved. They were riffing on flow.

**Construction.** `Windsurf`: a compound of two common words, evoking effortless motion and being
carried by a force rather than fighting it — which is the actual emotional promise of an AI pair
programmer. No team thinking about IDEs reaches this, because IDE vocabulary is code/dev/build/ship.

**Outcome.** Immediate market traction, memorable, searchable, spellable.

**Lesson.** The blind-brief mechanic is not theater. If you only run the true brief, you get the
category's existing vocabulary rearranged. Run the displaced pass first and run it honestly.

## Vercel (formerly Zeit)

**Situation.** A frontend/cloud platform whose definition of winning was "the most innovative,
fastest-moving company in the category."

**Insight.** Speed had to be in the *sound*, not in a claim. The name needed to feel alive and
daring before anyone read the docs.

**Construction.** A pure morpheme matrix: 55 prefixes × 102 suffixes, run three times over two
weeks. The parts were chosen for charge — prefixes and suffixes signalling accuracy/speed/flow,
classical roots signalling reliability, and letters the sound strategy flagged as fast. Each round
yielded ~60 *ideas* (Placek is careful to call them ideas, not solutions). `Vercel` came out: six
letters, opening on **v** — the researchers' most-alive letter — familiar parts in an assembly that didn't
exist.

**Outcome.** A distinctive name in a category of descriptive ones.

**Lesson.** Invented names are built, not brainstormed. Combinatorics plus a sound strategy plus
multiple rounds beats free association. This is the case to imitate when the answer must be a
coined word.

## Sonos

**Situation.** Multi-room home audio, a new category needing to feel both premium and technical.

**Insight.** Rejected at first for "not being entertainment enough" — the client wanted warmth, the
name delivered precision.

**Construction.** `son-` (sound, via Latin *sonus*) doubled into a palindrome. The symmetry is a
processing-fluency device: cheap to read, cheap to store, reads the same in reverse — and even
survives being flipped upside down, which the logo exploits. Nasals and a back vowel give it
roundness and fullness, the **o**/**m**-family weight.

**Outcome.** A category-defining brand.

**Lesson.** Structural symmetry is a legitimate memorability lever, and "the client's first reaction
was negative" appears in nearly every one of these stories. Note this one honestly when presenting:
the discomfort is real and sometimes the client is right — the argument for shipping anyway is
strategic fit, not stubbornness.

## Navan (formerly TripActions)

**Situation.** A corporate travel company expanding into expense management and financial services.
`TripActions` was descriptive and accurate — and therefore a cage. A company with "trip" in its name
cannot credibly sell a corporate card.

**Insight.** The name had to survive the company's next three business lines, not describe the
current one.

**Construction.** `Navan` — a palindrome, echoing *navigation* without being about travel. Energetic
sounds, five letters, symmetrical, semantically open.

**Outcome.** $500M+ revenue after the rebrand, with the new lines credible under one name.

**Lesson.** Descriptive names have a delayed cost that arrives exactly when the company succeeds
enough to expand. Ask what the name forbids in five years, not what it explains today.

## Febreze (Procter & Gamble)

**Situation.** An odor-eliminating fabric spray — a new benefit consumers had no vocabulary for.

**Construction.** `Febreze` = `fab(ric)` + `breeze`, spelled to be ownable. Fricatives throughout
(**f**, **z**) carry air, lightness and flow, which is literally the product mechanism; the front
vowels keep it light rather than heavy or chemical.

**Lesson.** Phonetic invention anchored to two real words: the listener decodes it instantly without
ever having seen it, and it still clears trademark. When a product's benefit has a physical
sensation, put the sensation in the consonants.

## Impossible Burger (Impossible Foods)

**Situation.** Plant-based meat, a category whose existing names were apologetic and descriptive
(veggie burger, meat substitute, meat-free) — every one of them framing the product as a lesser
version of something else.

**Construction.** A transplanted real word carrying the *company's ambition* rather than the
product's ingredients. Long, and long is fine. `Impossible` reframes the product as a technical feat
instead of a compromise, and it's a word with existing emotional charge that nobody in food had
claimed.

**Lesson.** When the category's default vocabulary is defensive, the name is the cheapest place to
reframe. Also: nothing about the name mentions plants, meat, or health — and that's the point.

## Outback (Subaru)

**Situation.** A wagon that needed to read as capable and adventurous without being a truck.

**Construction.** A transplanted geography word: instantly visualizable, no learning cost, back
vowels giving weight and ruggedness, and a compound of two everyday words. Placek uses it as the
accessibility example — *"the human brain doesn't like complexity, or remember it very well."*

**Lesson.** Accessibility is a real criterion, not a fallback. A name that produces an image in one
hearing has already won half the memorability fight. Geography, weather and landscape are
under-mined territories for exactly this reason.

## Cross-cutting patterns

1. **The client hated it first** — Azure, BlackBerry, Sonos, Pentium. Consistent enough to treat as
   a feature of good names, not a coincidence.
2. **The insight is almost never about words.** It's about a behavior (nobody likes mopping), a
   constraint (numbers can't be trademarked), or a future (we'll sell financial services).
3. **The winning name usually comes from outside the category** — flow, sky, fruit, geography, the
   periodic table.
4. **Descriptive names lose on a delay.** They win the first meeting and cost you the expansion.
5. **Length matters far less than assumed.** Impossible Burger, BlackBerry, PowerBook, Windsurf.
   Density of association beats brevity.
