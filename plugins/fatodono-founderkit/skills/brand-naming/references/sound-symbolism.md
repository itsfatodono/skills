# Sound Symbolism Reference

Sound symbolism is the finding that speech sounds carry meaning-like associations *before* semantics
— that `kiki` feels spiky and `bouba` feels round to people across languages and writing systems.
The researchers built their practice on this: reportedly $2-3M and five years of cross-cultural research,
directed by Will Leben (Stanford linguistics), supported by a network of 100+ linguists in
75 countries.

Read the honesty note at the bottom before writing rationales. The single fastest way to discredit
a naming recommendation is to over-claim what a letter "means."

---

## 1. Vowels — the strongest and best-replicated effect

The core split is **front vowels** (tongue forward) vs. **back vowels** (tongue back). Klink (2000)
tested this with 124 nonsense word pairs differing on exactly one phonetic dimension, and the
pattern has replicated across US, European and Asian samples.

| | **Front vowels** — /i/ (bee), /ɪ/ (bit), /eɪ/ (bait), /ɛ/ (bet), /æ/ (bat) | **Back vowels** — /u/ (boot), /ʊ/ (book), /oʊ/ (boat), /ɔ/ (bought), /ɑ/ (father) |
|---|---|---|
| Size | smaller | larger |
| Weight | lighter | heavier |
| Color | lighter | darker |
| Speed | faster | slower |
| Texture | thinner, sharper, milder | thicker, duller, richer |
| Temperature | colder | warmer |
| Force | weaker | more potent |
| Gender coding | more feminine | more masculine |
| Affect | prettier, friendlier | more serious, more durable |

Working examples: `Pepsi`, `Nike`, `Fitbit`, `Slim Jim` (front — light, quick, small) vs. `Volvo`,
`Toro`, `Ford`, `Google`, `Roomba` (back — solid, large, grounded).

**Use:** decide the size/weight/speed of the thing you're naming, then bias the *stressed* vowel
accordingly. A precision instrument wants front vowels; a heavy-duty truck wants back vowels; a
brand that needs both (fast *and* substantial) can split them across syllables — `BlackBerry` puts
the weight in `black` and the speed in the short-e of `berry`.

## 2. Consonant manner — stops vs. fricatives

| | **Stops / plosives** — p b t d k g | **Fricatives** — f v s z θ ð ʃ ʒ |
|---|---|---|
| Perceived as | larger, heavier, slower, more potent | smaller, faster, lighter, softer |
| Feel | hard edges, impact, confidence, punch | air, flow, glide, continuity |
| Recall | **word-initial stops are the strongest single lever for brand recall and recognition** | weaker for recall, stronger for texture |

Stops: `Coca-Cola`, `PayPal`, `TikTok`, `Kodak`, `BlackBerry`, `Pentium`, `Dasani`.
Fricatives: `Swiffer`, `Febreze`, `Vercel`, `Azure`, `Sonos`, `Zoom`.

If the brand needs to be *remembered from one exposure* (consumer retail, mass advertising), an
initial stop is close to a free win. If it needs to feel effortless and flowing (services, software,
anything selling ease), fricatives carry that better — and can be paired with an initial stop to get
both, which is exactly the `Dasani` shape: stop → fricatives → light front vowel.

## 3. Voicing

| | **Voiceless** — p t k f s ʃ | **Voiced** — b d g v z ʒ |
|---|---|---|
| | smaller, lighter, sharper, faster, crisper | larger, heavier, duller, rounder, more potent |

Voiceless reads as precise and clean (`Kit Kat`, `Crisp`, `Post-it`); voiced reads as substantial and
warm (`Bugaboo`, `Guggenheim`, `Volvo`).

## 4. Sonorants — liquids and nasals

- **Liquids /l/ /r/** glide with no obstruction: smoothness, ease, continuity, elegance.
  `Lululemon`, `Lyra`, `Rolls-Royce`, `Vercel`.
- **Nasals /m/ /n/** resonate: fullness, warmth, hum, comfort, maternal associations.
  `Mmm`, `Nutella`, `Amazon`, `Sonos`. The researchers specifically flag **o** and **m** as the
  fullness/weight pair.
- **Sibilants /s/ /z/ /ʃ/** are speed and precision in small doses; in excess they read hissy or
  cheap. One is a feature, three is a liability.
- **Affricates /tʃ/ /dʒ/** (`ch`, `j`) hold then release: energy with a kick. `Chipotle`, `Jaguar`.

## 5. The researchers' own letter-level claims

House doctrine from their proprietary research, as stated publicly by Placek. These are the claims
the firm actually sells; treat them as informed priors rather than published findings (see honesty
note).

| Letter | Claimed signal | Their examples |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | reliability, dependability — "one of the most reliable sounds in English" | BlackBerry, BlackRock, Timberland |
| **T** | reliability, solidity | T-Mobile, Timberland |
| **V** | alive, vital, daring | Corvette, Viagra, Virgin, Vercel |
| **Z** | attention, magnetism, signal | Azure |
| **X** | innovation, technology, the new | (category-wide) |
| **O, M** | fullness, weight, roundness | Sonos, Amazon |
| **P, K, B, D, Z** | power and speed cluster | Pentium, Kodak |
| **short e** | speed | BlackBerry (`-berry`) |
| **str-** | slow, effortful — a cluster to avoid when selling immediacy | "Strawberry" (rejected) |

## 6. Structural devices

Sound is one layer; how the name is *built* is the other. The researchers' finalists are almost always one
of these:

**Compound of two known words.** `BlackBerry`, `PowerBook`, `Impossible Burger`, `Windsurf`,
`Outback`. Two known concepts generate a denser associative network than one — Placek's "1 + 1 = 3."
Longer than a coined word and usually more memorable anyway, which is why length caps are a bad
filter.

**Root graft.** Take a root with borrowed authority and fuse it to a genre-marking suffix.
`Pentium` = `penta-` (five, the 5th-gen chip) + `-ium` (the suffix of sodium, uranium, titanium — it
makes the chip read as a fundamental element rather than a part number). `Dasani` = crisp `da-` +
Latin `san` (health) + light `-i`. This is the highest-leverage technique for inventing a name that
feels like it already existed.

**Transplanted real word.** Take a word with the right feeling from a completely different domain.
`Azure`, `Apple`, `Outback`, `Impossible`, `Oracle`, `Windsurf`. Maximum processing fluency (zero
learning cost) plus an owned metaphor. Trademark is harder inside your own category and easier
outside it — which is the whole point of transplanting.

**Phonetic invention anchored to a real word.** `Swiffer` (swift/sweep/swipe), `Febreze`
(fabric + breeze), `Fios` (fiber + optics). Sounds like a word you know, isn't one, clears legal
more easily.

**Palindrome / symmetry.** `Navan`, `Sonos`, `Civic`. Placek's argument is processing efficiency —
the brain handles the symmetry cheaply, so it sticks. Navan also bought category freedom: a travel
company named TripActions couldn't credibly sell financial services; Navan could, and did
($500M+ revenue after the rebrand).

**Truncation / blend.** `Vercel`, `Cisco` (San Francisco), `Intel`. Familiar parts, unfamiliar
assembly — the "surprisingly familiar" target.

## 7. Processing fluency — the governing constraint

*"The brain is lazy. It likes the familiar."* The target is **surprising, but surprisingly
familiar**:

- Familiar **parts**, unfamiliar **assembly**.
- Pronounceable on first sight by a non-native speaker.
- Spellable after hearing it once (this is a hard test — apply it honestly).
- Visualizable. The researchers' own rule: *"All of us remember what we understand and what we can
  visualize."* `BlackBerry` and `Outback` produce an image; `Synergix` produces nothing.

Fluency and distinctiveness pull against each other, and the good names sit in the tension: familiar
enough to process instantly, strange enough that you notice you processed it.

## 8. How much of this is actually established

Be straight with users about this; it makes the recommendation stronger, not weaker.

**Well supported.** The vowel size/weight/speed effects and the stop-vs-fricative and voicing
contrasts are replicated across many studies and multiple language families (Sapir 1929; Klink 2000;
Yorkston & Menon 2004; Lowrey & Shrum 2007), and the bouba/kiki effect is one of the more robust
findings in psycholinguistics. Initial-stop advantage for brand recall is well documented.

**Real but modest.** Effect sizes are small-to-moderate and measured mostly on *nonsense* words in
forced-choice tasks. Once a real name accumulates meaning through use, semantics and association
swamp phonetics — nothing about the sound of `Apple` predicted what it came to mean.

**House doctrine, not published science.** The letter-level claims in §5 come from the researchers'
unpublished proprietary studies. They are plausible, internally consistent, and backed by a
40-year commercial record — and they are not peer-reviewed findings. Say "the b sound tests as
reliable in one naming firm's research" rather than "the letter b means reliability."

**The practical stance:** sound symbolism is a *design* tool and a *tiebreaker*, not a predictor of
success. Use it to shape the candidate pool before generation and to choose between finalists that
are otherwise equal on strategy. Never use it as the primary argument for a name that fails on
strategy, memorability, or legal.
